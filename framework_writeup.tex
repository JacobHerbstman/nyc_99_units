\section{The question and the minimum model}

The evidence to explain is a sharp increase in 99-unit New Building filings
after 485-x. The first economic question is narrow:

\begin{quote}
How costly must crossing the 100-unit threshold be for a developer to remove
units, redesign a project, or organize a larger development as multiple
sub-100-unit eligible sites?
\end{quote}

Each developer begins with a preferred project in a counterfactual world in
which the provisions that change at 100 units are smoothly continued from
below the threshold. If the preferred project reaches 100 units, the developer
may:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Bunch}: choose the best feasible unit count below 100;
  \item \textbf{Cross}: choose the best feasible unit count at or above 100
  and incur the signed net threshold wedge; or
  \item \textbf{Split}: choose a legally and physically feasible configuration
  with multiple sub-100 eligible sites, possibly including unequal or hybrid
  building sizes.
\end{enumerate}

This setup links observed responses to three dollar-valued objects: the loss
from changing scale, the net cost or benefit of crossing, and the incremental
cost of a split configuration. It does not assume that 99 is physically or
legally feasible on every site.

\section{Institutional details that determine measurement}

\subsection{The legal threshold is not the DOB building}

Real Property Tax Law Section 485-x defines an \emph{eligible site} as either
a tax lot containing an eligible multiple dwelling or a zoning lot containing
two or more eligible multiple dwellings that are part of a single application.
A large rental project is an eligible site with at least 100 residential
units.\footnote{See
\href{https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPT/485-X}{New York Real
Property Tax Law Section 485-x}, especially subdivisions 1(s) and 1(bb).}
Three distinct units must therefore be reconstructed:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item the DOB root job and physical building;
  \item the legal 485-x eligible site at filing and commencement; and
  \item the parent economic development opportunity over which scale and
  configuration were chosen.
\end{enumerate}

Multiple DOB filings on one tax lot are not automatically multiple eligible
sites. Multiple buildings on a zoning lot can also be aggregated when they are
part of one 485-x application. Conversely, distinct tax lots and applications
may permit separate eligible sites, but neither a current BBL nor a filing
count proves that result. HPD's Option A/B application asks about the eligible
site, buildings, lots, units, commencement, and wage compliance, making the
HPD docket and application central validation records.\footnote{See HPD's
\href{https://www.nyc.gov/assets/hpd/downloads/pdfs/services/485-x-Application.pdf}
{485-x Options A and B application}.}

The structural observation should ultimately be one parent opportunity, with
one weight per opportunity. Legal-site and building records are nested
outcomes, not independent observations.

\subsection{Filing, amendment, treatment, and completion are different dates}

DOB's initial filing records the project proposed at filing. A post-approval
amendment can change that proposal, and neither record is necessarily the final
built outcome. Permit and completion dates are separate again. Policy exposure
depends on the statutory commencement rules, not simply the filing year.

The empirical data must therefore preserve, rather than overwrite:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the initial filing and each observed amendment;
  \item the unit count at each stage;
  \item filing, approval, permit, commencement, and completion dates;
  \item rental tenure and actual or intended 485-x participation; and
  \item tax-lot and zoning-lot structure as of the relevant dates.
\end{itemize}

DOB NOW provides the recent filing and amendment history, while HDB extends the
unit-count history. BIS and older DOB records will be needed for a consistent
long preperiod. No source should silently replace another when their BBLs,
BINs, dates, or units disagree.

\subsection{The wage object is a project-specific employer-cost increment}

The 100-unit requirement is not a generic union/nonunion wage differential.
For each trade $r$, time period $t$, and hour type $o$, let
$\bar c^{\mathrm{comp}}_{rto}$ be required creditable compensation and
$c^0_{irto}$ be the creditable compensation that project $i$ would
otherwise pay. A mechanical compliance-gap calculation is

\begin{equation}
\Delta C_i^{\mathrm{comp}}(n)
=
\sum_{r,t,o} H_{irto}(n)
\pos{\bar c^{\mathrm{comp}}_{rto}-c^0_{irto}}.
\label{eq:compliance-gap}
\end{equation}

This is not yet the full employer-cost increment. Creditability rules matter:
qualifying supplements can satisfy part of the requirement, while payroll
taxes and several statutory employer charges do not.\footnote{See the New York
City Comptroller's
\href{https://comptroller.nyc.gov/services/for-the-public/workers-rights/485-x/}
{485-x regulations and frequently asked questions}. The guidance also makes
clear that rates can depend on classification and the applicable schedule.}
The structural wage cost is therefore

\begin{equation}
W_i(n)
=
\min_{p\in\mathcal P_i(n)} C_i^{\mathrm{employer,rule}}(n,p)
-C_i^{\mathrm{employer},0}(n),
\label{eq:wage-cost}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal P_i(n)$ is the set of lawful compensation packages that satisfy
the wage-and-supplement rule. Equation \eqref{eq:compliance-gap} is a
transparent first calculation; equation \eqref{eq:wage-cost} is the preferred
economic object. Both require covered hours, trade mix, timing, overtime or
shift exposure, counterfactual pay and benefits, and induced employer charges.

\subsection{Crossing creates a signed net wedge}

Let the signed present-value wedge at $n\geq100$ be

\begin{equation}
T_i(n)
=W_i(n)+A_i(n)+F_i(n)+K_i(n)-B_i(n),
\label{eq:cost-ledger}
\end{equation}

where $A_i$ is the incremental affordability cost, $F_i$ the incremental
filing fee, $K_i$ incremental administration and compliance, and $B_i$ the
incremental property-tax benefit. Each term is relative to the no-notch
benchmark and measured as a project-specific net present value. Obligations
common to the below- and above-threshold project do not belong in $T_i(n)$.

The word \emph{signed} matters. A more generous tax benefit may make
$T_i(n)<0$ for some projects. The threshold identifies the combined net
wedge. Behavioral bunching alone cannot isolate the construction-wage effect;
doing so requires separately measuring the ledger components. Projects at 150
or more units in the relevant zones must use the applicable additional wage
schedule rather than extrapolating the 100-unit rule.

\section{What the current audit establishes}

\subsection{The four descriptive objects}

The first audit plots:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item unit histograms for individual filings and provisional sites;
  \item total construction floor area against units;
  \item gross construction square feet per unit; and
  \item building and application counts per provisional site.
\end{enumerate}

The application and parent-opportunity distributions must remain separate.
Mass at 198 or 396 in a parent total is evidence about configuration choice; it
is not a 198- or 396-unit legal site unless the eligible-site crosswalk says so.

\subsection{Record-level audit of all 52 exact-99 filings}

The current DOB initial-file extract contains 52 distinct 2025 root jobs and
52 distinct initial BINs proposing exactly 99 units, spread across 43 filing
BBLs. A reproducible casebook retains one row per job and links each job to its
candidate parent, observed amendments, HDB record, physical fields, external
evidence, and next required record.

The present decomposition is:

\begin{itemize}
  \item 26 filings belong to ten candidate parents containing multiple
  99-unit buildings;
  \item seven have one 99-unit building plus non-99 companion buildings; and
  \item 19 are currently unlinked one-building filings.
\end{itemize}

Thus 33 of 52 exact-99 buildings are in a candidate multi-building parent under
the stated audit rule. The 52 buildings collapse to 36 candidate parent
opportunities: 19 unlinked singletons, seven mixed groups, and ten repeated-99
groups. Because the data currently end during 2026, only 19 anchors have a
complete 365-day forward companion window; 11 of those 19 are multi-building.
The full-sample 63.5 percent is therefore descriptive, not a population split
rate.

The repeated-99 parent totals are six groups at 198, one at 286, one at 367,
one at 396, and one at 591 units. These are candidate \emph{parent} totals.
Several groups also include non-99 buildings, so a model containing only equal
99+99 splits would miss observed hybrid configurations.

The records provide several checks on interpretation:

\begin{itemize}
  \item none of the 52 exact-99 filings is currently withdrawn;
  \item 29 have an observed post-approval amendment, all 29 continue to report
  99 units, and none shows an observed amendment-stage unit change;
  \item all 52 match HDB on units, while six disagree on BBL and 11 disagree on
  BIN across source snapshots;
  \item DOB descriptions contain explicit companion-job references in some
  groups and a shared project code across the six-building Queens group; and
  \item public reporting describes the Atlantic Avenue/Havens Place pair as
  one development with two 99-unit buildings, while earlier reporting shows a
  unified 261-unit proposal on the site that now contains the four Empire-area
  99-unit filings. These facts corroborate a common economic opportunity, not
  separate legal eligible sites.\footnote{See
  \href{https://commercialobserver.com/2025/07/developer-apartment-buildings-bed-stuy/}
  {Commercial Observer on 2433 Atlantic Avenue and 28 Havens Place} and
  \href{https://www.brownstoner.com/development/crown-heights-rezoning-73-empire-boulevard-city-planning-vote/}
  {Brownstoner on the earlier 73--99 Empire Boulevard proposal}.}
\end{itemize}

These findings overturn a loose characterization of 99-unit filings as mostly
small isolated buildings. Median gross construction area is approximately
81,126 square feet among currently unlinked 99s, 77,560 among repeated-99
cases, and 72,138 among 99s with other companions; the median is 12 stories in
all three groups. The data also do not establish that 99-unit buildings have
larger apartments. Gross construction area includes common, mechanical,
parking, and nonresidential space.

A targeted commercial real estate source review of all ten repeated-99 parents
adds a useful evidence ranking. Atlantic Avenue/Havens Place has direct
reporting of 485-x wage avoidance. The 165th Street and Empire Boulevard groups
have very strong circumstantial evidence: the former is publicly described as
a 591-unit zoning-lot development with a 99-unit component, while the latter
moved from a publicly described unified 261-unit plan to four 99-unit filings.
Three other parents have strong circumstantial evidence because DOB explicitly
calls the paired buildings one zoning lot; four remain suggestive based on
common filing, owner, architect, timing, and parcel evidence.\footnote{For the
Jamaica group, see New York YIMBY's
\href{https://newyorkyimby.com/2025/07/permits-filed-for-89-53-165th-street-in-jamaica-queens.html}
{report on 89-53 165th Street and the 591-unit zoning lot}. This ranking concerns
the economic configuration, not validated HPD eligible-site treatment.}

\section{The minimal static model}

\subsection{The no-notch value envelope and feasible unit set}

Let $d$ contain the full design: units, floor area, unit mix, amenities,
building count, and other physical and financial choices. Let
$\mathcal D_i(n)$ be project $i$'s feasible designs with $n$ units. Define

\begin{equation}
\widetilde\Pi_i^0(n)
=
\max_{d\in\mathcal D_i(n)}\Pi_i^0(d),
\qquad n\in\mathcal N_i,
\label{eq:value-envelope}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal N_i$ is a project-specific discrete feasible-unit set.
The counterfactual removes only provisions that change at 100, smoothly
continuing the below-threshold rules. It does not remove all 485-x obligations.
Let $n_i^0\in\arg\max_{n\in\mathcal N_i}\widetilde\Pi_i^0(n)$.

For transparent local algebra, use

\begin{equation}
\widetilde\Pi_i^0(n)
=
\overline\Pi_i-\frac{\gamma_i}{2}(n-n_i^0)^2,
\qquad \gamma_i>0.
\label{eq:quadratic-profit}
\end{equation}

The common-$\gamma$ model is the first benchmark, not a claim that redesign costs are
identical. Observable heterogeneity or a parsimonious random coefficient can
be added if the data reject it. The quadratic is local around 100; it is not a
complete model of opportunities with parent totals near 200--600 or of the
separate 150-unit schedule.

The value envelope makes unit-count choice tractable, but it does not by itself
generate floor area, unit size, or amenities. Those outcomes are held-out
validation moments unless we add a structural design module. Let
$d_i^{0*}(n)$ denote the optimizing design if we later model that mapping.

\subsection{Bunching}

The best feasible subthreshold count and its deterministic loss are

\begin{align}
n_i^B
&\in\arg\min_{n\in\mathcal N_i,\ n\leq99}
\frac{\gamma_i}{2}(n-n_i^0)^2,
\label{eq:bunch-choice}\\
L_i^B
&=
\frac{\gamma_i}{2}(n_i^B-n_i^0)^2.
\label{eq:bunch-loss}
\end{align}

If 99 is feasible and $n_i^0\geq100$, then $n_i^B=99$. Otherwise the model
uses the closest feasible subthreshold count; it does not manufacture a
99-unit option.

\subsection{Crossing}

The developer solves

\begin{align}
n_i^C
&\in\arg\min_{n\in\mathcal N_i,\ n\geq100}
\left\{
\frac{\gamma_i}{2}(n-n_i^0)^2+T_i(n)
\right\},
\label{eq:cross-choice}\\
L_i^C
&=
\frac{\gamma_i}{2}(n_i^C-n_i^0)^2+T_i(n_i^C).
\label{eq:cross-loss}
\end{align}

For algebra only, relax integer feasibility and approximate the local wedge as

\begin{equation}
T_i(n)=\alpha_i+\tau_i(n-100),
\qquad \alpha_i=T_i(100).
\label{eq:affine-cost}
\end{equation}

Then

\begin{equation}
n_i^C=\max\left\{100,n_i^0-\frac{\tau_i}{\gamma_i}\right\}.
\label{eq:cross-size}
\end{equation}

If the solution is interior,

\begin{equation}
L_i^C
=
\alpha_i+\tau_i(n_i^0-100)-\frac{\tau_i^2}{2\gamma_i}.
\label{eq:cross-loss-interior}
\end{equation}

If the lower bound binds,

\begin{equation}
L_i^C
=
\frac{\gamma_i}{2}(n_i^0-100)^2+\alpha_i.
\label{eq:cross-loss-boundary}
\end{equation}

This centering makes the comparative statics clear. The fixed component
$\alpha_i$ changes whether the project crosses but does not change the
interior crossing size. The slope $\tau_i$ changes both crossing probability
and conditional crossing size.

\subsection{Splitting as a configuration choice}

Let $\mathcal Q_i$ be the set of legally and physically feasible split
configurations. A configuration $q$ contains $m(q)\geq2$ eligible sites
with site-level counts $\mathbf n_q=(n_{q1},\ldots,n_{qm})$, each below 100,
and may contain other buildings or shared infrastructure. Let
$K_{iq}^{S}$ include the incremental present-value cost of subdivision,
financing, applications, duplicated cores and systems, coordination, and lost
usable area. Its loss is

\begin{equation}
L_{iq}^{S}
=
\frac{\gamma_i}{2}
\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m(q)}n_{q\ell}-n_i^0\right)^2
+K_{iq}^{S},
\qquad q\in\mathcal Q_i,
\label{eq:split-configuration-loss}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
L_i^S=\min_{q\in\mathcal Q_i}L_{iq}^{S}.
\label{eq:split-loss}
\end{equation}

This formulation includes 99+99, unequal splits, and hybrid parents. For a
first benchmark, one can set $K_{iq}^{S}=(m(q)-1)K^S$, but that restriction
should be tested rather than built into the definition. If no valid
configuration is feasible, $S$ is absent. A candidate repeated-99 cluster is
not coded as $S$ until the legal-site crosswalk validates it.

\subsection{i.i.d. choice shocks}

Let $\mathcal R_i\subseteq\{B,C,S\}$ be the feasible response set. Response
$r$ yields

\begin{equation}
V_{ir}=\overline\Pi_i-L_i^r+\sigma\varepsilon_{ir},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{ir}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}
\text{Type-I extreme value}.
\label{eq:random-value}
\end{equation}

The shocks represent genuine response-specific profit heterogeneity observed
by the developer but not the econometrician. They should not be described as a
license for measurement error or model misspecification. Choice probabilities
are

\begin{equation}
P_i(r)=
\frac{\exp(-L_i^r/\sigma)}
{\sum_{j\in\mathcal R_i}\exp(-L_i^j/\sigma)}.
\label{eq:logit-probability}
\end{equation}

The common value $\overline\Pi_i$ cancels because the model conditions on an
observed opportunity. The i.i.d. assumption imposes IIA. It remains the simple
baseline requested here; a nested-logit specification grouping bunching and
splitting is a later robustness check, not part of the core estimator.

The index $i$ must refer to one economic development opportunity, not every DOB
filing attached to it. At the filing level, residuals remain strongly correlated
within the same feature lot, and provisional site aggregation lowers held-out
prediction error. Treating those component filings as separate developers with
i.i.d. shocks would turn filing architecture and duplicate records into economic
heterogeneity. The legal-site and parent crosswalk therefore comes before the
i.i.d. assumption: the simple model keeps independent shocks across validated
opportunities, not across their component applications.

\section{Algebra and comparative statics}

\subsection{Individual odds, not aggregate inversion}

At a given project state,

\begin{align}
\log\frac{P_i(B)}{P_i(C)}&=\frac{L_i^C-L_i^B}{\sigma},\\
\log\frac{P_i(S)}{P_i(C)}&=\frac{L_i^C-L_i^S}{\sigma},\\
\log\frac{P_i(S)}{P_i(B)}&=\frac{L_i^B-L_i^S}{\sigma}.
\label{eq:pairwise-odds}
\end{align}

These are individual-project relationships. With heterogeneous costs, feasible
sets, and latent $n_i^0$, the log of an aggregate share ratio is not the
average loss difference divided by $\sigma$. Estimation must integrate the
project-level probabilities before comparing them with aggregate frequencies.

For any response $k$,

\begin{align}
\frac{\partial P_i(k)}{\partial L_i^k}
&=-\frac{P_i(k)[1-P_i(k)]}{\sigma}<0,\\
\frac{\partial P_i(r)}{\partial L_i^k}
&=\frac{P_i(r)P_i(k)}{\sigma}>0,
\qquad r\neq k.
\label{eq:probability-derivatives}
\end{align}

\subsection{Threshold level and slope}

Because $\partial L_i^C/\partial\alpha_i=1$,

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_i}
\log\frac{P_i(B)}{P_i(C)}
=
\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_i}
\log\frac{P_i(S)}{P_i(C)}
=\frac{1}{\sigma}.
\label{eq:alpha-cs}
\end{equation}

The envelope theorem gives

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial L_i^C}{\partial\tau_i}=n_i^C-100.
\label{eq:tau-loss}
\end{equation}

Thus a steeper schedule increases bunching and splitting relative to crossing
when the chosen crossing project exceeds 100. For an interior crosser,

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial n_i^C}{\partial\tau_i}=-\frac{1}{\gamma_i}.
\label{eq:tau-size}
\end{equation}

The fixed wedge shifts crossing probability; the slope also shrinks the
conditional size of crossing projects.

\subsection{Design rigidity and split cost}

Let $d_i^B=n_i^0-n_i^B$ and $d_i^C=n_i^0-n_i^C$. Then

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma_i}
\log\frac{P_i(B)}{P_i(C)}
=
\frac{(d_i^C)^2-(d_i^B)^2}{2\sigma}.
\label{eq:gamma-odds}
\end{equation}

When crossing preserves more of the preferred scale, a larger curvature makes
bunching less attractive. Increasing $K_{iq}^{S}$ raises the loss of that
configuration one for one; expanding $\mathcal Q_i$ weakly lowers $L_i^S$.

\subsection{Deterministic frontiers and their domains}

The shocks remove a literal marginal buncher, but zero-shock frontiers are
useful summaries. Suppose 99 is feasible, $n_i^0\geq100$, and crossing costs
a constant $T_i$ while preserving $n_i^0$. Bunching and crossing tie at

\begin{equation}
n_i^0=99+\sqrt{\frac{2T_i}{\gamma_i}}.
\label{eq:simple-frontier}
\end{equation}

For an exposed project this requires $T_i\geq\gamma_i/2$; if $T_i<0$,
crossing strictly dominates bunching in this simple comparison.

Under the affine schedule and an interior crossing solution, define
$d_i=n_i^0-99$. The bunch--cross frontier is

\begin{equation}
d_i
=
\frac{\tau_i}{\gamma_i}
+\sqrt{\frac{2(\alpha_i-\tau_i)}{\gamma_i}}.
\label{eq:affine-frontier}
\end{equation}

This expression requires $\alpha_i-\tau_i\geq0$ and must also satisfy the
interior condition $n_i^0-\tau_i/\gamma_i>100$. If the crossing lower bound
binds, the frontier is

\begin{equation}
d_i=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha_i}{\gamma_i},
\label{eq:boundary-frontier}
\end{equation}

subject to the boundary condition and $d_i\geq1$. These restrictions prevent
the closed-form expressions from being applied outside their economic domain.

\section{Estimation and simulation roadmap}

\subsection{Chunk 1: finish the outcome crosswalk}

The 52-row casebook is the starting point, not the final outcome data. For each
exact-99 building and each companion, establish:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item initial, amendment, permit, and completion-stage units;
  \item rental tenure and intended or realized 485-x participation;
  \item tax lots and zoning lot at filing and commencement;
  \item HPD registration, docket, application, and eligible-site units; and
  \item beneficial owner, financing, and parent development opportunity.
\end{enumerate}

The first records requests should prioritize the 26 repeated-99 cases, the two
99-unit anchors in high-priority different-owner pairs, and the seven mixed
parents. The 19 unlinked cases remain ``currently unlinked,'' not proven
standalone projects; ten are right-censored by the companion window.

Outcome coding occurs only after validation:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $B$: a sub-100 legal site with posterior mass on $n_i^0\geq100$;
  \item $C$: a legal site that crosses 100; and
  \item $S$: one parent opportunity realized as multiple legally distinct
  sub-100 eligible sites.
\end{itemize}

Classification sensitivity should be reported by evidence tier. This is a
diagnostic, not a partial-identification exercise.

\subsection{Chunk 2: estimate the local no-notch distribution}

The counterfactual object is the total number of units the parent development
opportunity would have chosen without the 100-unit notch. If one 198-unit
opportunity becomes two 99-unit buildings, the observed filing counts are 99
and 99 but the relevant counterfactual scale is 198. Estimating the model on
individual filings would therefore answer the wrong question.

For each parent, estimate

\begin{equation}
F_0(n_i^0\mid X_i,t_i;\eta)
\label{eq:baseline-distribution}
\end{equation}

using a local, policy-relevant preperiod, explicit calendar-time controls, and
one weight per parent. The current enhanced parent joins filings using
conservative pre-filing links and corroborated exact tax-lot adjacency. Exact
MapPLUTO polygons are available consistently only from 2019 onward, so the
production model uses 2019--2023 and does not impute earlier adjacency. The
legal eligible-site crosswalk remains unfinished; ``enhanced parent'' is an
economic-opportunity proxy, not an HPD determination.

For each post-policy parent, retain the full posterior or simulation
distribution of $n_i^0$. Do not deterministically label a 99 as a buncher
because a point prediction exceeds 99. Total construction area may be modeled
jointly or conditionally; gross square feet per unit is derived, not a third
independent outcome.

The production benchmark is a linear log-unit model with one static scale
shock per parent:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\log n_i^0={}&
\beta_0+
\beta_1\log(\text{lot area}_i)+
\beta_2\text{ResidFAR}_i+
\beta_3\text{BuiltFAR}_i \\
&+\beta_4(t_i-\bar t)+Z_i'\gamma+u_i,
\qquad
u_i\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\tau^2).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:no-notch-log-units}
\end{equation}

Here $Z_i$ contains borough, zoning group, and prior site use, while
$\beta_4(t_i-\bar t)$ is a linear filing-year control. Lot area is summed across unique pre-filing
lots; FAR measures are lot-area weighted. Missing residential FAR has its own
indicator. The parameter $\tau$ measures residual dispersion in preferred
no-notch scale. It is not the dollar-valued response shock $\sigma$ in the
bunch-cross-split model.

Because the estimation sample begins at six units, ordinary least squares on
the selected sample does not recover the maintained distribution. For an
integer unit count $n$ and estimation floor $c=6$, the benchmark instead
maximizes the rounded, lower-truncated probability

\begin{equation}
\Pr(n_i^0=n\mid n_i^0\geq c,X_i)
=
\frac{
\Phi\!\left(\frac{\log(n+0.5)-X_i'\beta}{\tau}\right)
-
\Phi\!\left(\frac{\log(n-0.5)-X_i'\beta}{\tau}\right)
}{
1-
\Phi\!\left(\frac{\log(c-0.5)-X_i'\beta}{\tau}\right)
}.
\label{eq:rounded-truncated-units}
\end{equation}

Forward validation compares filing models, conservative-parent models, and the
enhanced-parent model on the same enhanced-parent outcomes in 2021--2023. The
2019--2023 enhanced-parent model has the best average rounded log score, local
CDF fit, and 100-plus share calibration. The gain is modest and not uniform:
the long-history filing model performs better in the short 2021 window, while
the recent parent models improve substantially in 2022 and 2023. This evidence
supports the simpler parent model as the working specification, not as an
unchallenged final model. Filing-level models, the 2010--2023 history, the
11-unit floor, richer covariates, placebo years, and bootstrap uncertainty
remain prespecified audits.

The working estimates use 2,040 eligible parents from 2019--2023 and score 583
parents in 2025, containing 609 component filings. Figure
\ref{fig:parent-counterfactual} compares their observed distribution with the
fitted no-notch distribution. There are 35 observed exact-99 parents but only
0.87 expected without the notch, an excess of 34.1. The model predicts 84.7
parents at or above 100 but observes 61, a missing mass of 23.7. Thus the two
mass-balance moments disagree by 10.4 parents. That gap is substantively
important: the model is a disciplined counterfactual benchmark, but it is not
yet a cost estimate.

Calibrating the exact-99 excess alone implies that affected no-notch projects
extend to roughly 160 units and average 125 units. Calibrating the missing
100-plus mass instead implies a frontier near 136 and an average near 116.
These are different summaries of the same misspecified mass balance, not a
range that should be averaged. The economically useful conclusion is narrower:
after reconstructing parent opportunities, the typical affected
counterfactual appears much closer to 120 units than to 170, even though the
upper tail includes parents whose total observed configuration is 198 units or
more.

\subsection{Chunk 3: build the threshold ledger}

Construct $W_i(n)$ from hours and counterfactual compensation, then add each
remaining term of \eqref{eq:cost-ledger}. Every ledger row needs:

\begin{itemize}
  \item a dollar NPV and explicit sign;
  \item source, vintage, and policy rule;
  \item scaling with units, area, trade mix, time, and location;
  \item whether it changes at 100 or is common on both sides; and
  \item missingness or imputation flags.
\end{itemize}

The wage-only counterfactual is meaningful only after the other threshold
changes are held fixed. The observed response identifies the net wedge, not the
wage component by itself.

\subsection{Chunk 4: estimate project-level response parameters}

With closed-form logit probabilities, there is no reason to simulate Gumbel
shocks. Integrate only over the latent no-notch state:

\begin{equation}
\Pr(y_i=r\mid X_i)
=
\int P_i(r\mid n^0,X_i;\theta)
\,dF_0(n^0\mid X_i;\widehat\eta).
\label{eq:integrated-probability}
\end{equation}

A simulated maximum-likelihood baseline is

\begin{equation}
\widehat\theta
=
\arg\max_{\theta}
\sum_i
\log\left[
\frac{1}{D}\sum_{d=1}^{D}
P_i(y_i\mid n_{id}^0,X_i;\theta)
\right],
\quad
n_{id}^0\sim F_0(\cdot\mid X_i;\widehat\eta).
\label{eq:simulated-likelihood}
\end{equation}

The first parameter vector can be
$\theta=(\gamma,K^S,\sigma)$, with measured project-specific ledger terms
entering directly. A parsimonious $K_{iq}^{S}=Z_{iq}'\beta_S$ and observed
heterogeneity in $\gamma_i$ come only after the validated sample supports
them. Bootstrap at the parent-opportunity level and re-estimate $F_0$ within
each bootstrap draw.

The core estimation outcomes are response category, site-level units, parent
configuration, and crossing size. Use exact one-unit bins near 99. Estimate
with policy-timing and placebo thresholds visible, rather than smoothing away
the notch.

\subsection{Chunk 5: identification and model checks}

Before interpreting costs, report:

\begin{itemize}
  \item likelihood or objective profiles for $\gamma,K^S,\sigma$;
  \item Hessian or Jacobian conditioning and parameter correlations;
  \item Monte Carlo recovery in samples with the actual sample size and
  covariate distribution;
  \item sensitivity to parent and legal-site classifications;
  \item pseudo-thresholds such as 89, 109, and 119 units;
  \item pseudo-policy dates and alternative local preperiods; and
  \item an IIA check, with nested logit only as a robustness extension.
\end{itemize}

The point-estimation baseline remains fully parametric. These exercises reveal
weak identification or misspecification without turning the first paper into a
partial-identification analysis.

\subsection{Chunk 6: held-out validation}

The model must match outcomes it actually generates: exact unit histograms,
bunch/cross/split shares, crossing sizes, parent totals, and configuration
counts. It should also face held-out physical diagnostics such as total area,
gross area per unit, stories, and building counts. A failure on those outcomes
is informative, but the baseline cannot be said to predict them until a design
mapping $d_i^{0*}(n)$ is estimated.

Large candidate parent totals cannot validate the local quadratic alone. They
test the split-configuration menu, parent measurement, and any wider-scale
profit module. Projects crossing 150 must be evaluated with the correct
schedule.

\section{Developer-profit and unit counterfactuals}

The first counterfactual removes only the measured 100-unit construction-
compensation increment:

\begin{equation}
T_i^{\mathrm{no\ wage}}(n)
=T_i^{\mathrm{policy}}(n)-W_i(n).
\label{eq:wage-counterfactual}
\end{equation}

Recompute integrated response probabilities, crossing sizes, configurations,
and expected units for the observed opportunity sample. This yields the
sample-conditional change in bunching, crossing, splitting, units, and private
developer value.

For Type-I extreme-value shocks, expected maximum value equals

\begin{equation}
\overline\Pi_i
+\sigma\log\left[
\sum_{r\in\mathcal R_i}\exp(-L_i^r/\sigma)
\right]
+\sigma\gamma_E,
\label{eq:inclusive-value}
\end{equation}

where $\gamma_E$ is Euler's constant under the standard shock normalization.
Both $\overline\Pi_i$ and $\sigma\gamma_E$ cancel in a within-project policy
difference. The developer-value gain from removing the wage component is

\begin{equation}
\Delta\Pi_i^{\mathrm{no\ wage}}
=
\sigma
\left\{
\log\sum_r\exp(-L_{ir}^{\mathrm{no\ wage}}/\sigma)
-
\log\sum_r\exp(-L_{ir}^{\mathrm{policy}}/\sigma)
\right\}.
\label{eq:developer-gain}
\end{equation}

Summing this expression across the modeled filing opportunities measures the
private burden on that observed margin. It is not a citywide developer-profit
effect and not social welfare: projects that never filed, workers, renters,
tax revenue, and general-equilibrium responses are absent.

\section{Concrete order of work}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Complete the 52-case audit.} Obtain HPD docket/application,
  zoning-lot, commencement-date, tenure, beneficial-owner, and completion
  evidence, beginning with repeated-99 and high-priority mixed cases.
  \item \textbf{Freeze the outcome crosswalk.} Produce building-, legal-site-,
  and parent-level datasets with evidence codes and symmetric pre/post rules.
  \item \textbf{Estimate a local no-notch distribution.} Use the validated
  opportunity as the observation and estimate
  \eqref{eq:rounded-truncated-units}. Use the expanding six-plus sample as the
  benchmark, retain the rolling five-year 11-plus model as the required sample
  robustness, and preserve posterior uncertainty in $n_i^0$. Revalidate both
  after the crosswalk is fixed and across pseudo-policy dates.
  \item \textbf{Build the wage module.} Report the compliance gap and full
  employer-cost increment separately for 99, 100, 120, and 149 units and for
  relevant trade, time, and location profiles.
  \item \textbf{Build the complete signed ledger.} Add affordability, fees,
  compliance, and incremental tax benefits as project-specific NPVs.
  \item \textbf{Estimate the simple response model.} Start with common
  $\gamma,K^S,\sigma$, exact feasible sets where observable, analytic logit
  probabilities, and simulated integration over $n_i^0$.
  \item \textbf{Diagnose before interpreting.} Use profiles, Monte Carlo
  recovery, timing/placebo tests, and classification sensitivity.
  \item \textbf{Run the wage-removal counterfactual.} Report restored units and
  sample-conditional developer value. Broader welfare follows later.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Bottom line}

The simplest defensible framework remains a static discrete-choice model of
bunching, crossing, and legally valid splitting. The revisions make that model
more literal: unit sets are discrete and project-specific; the crossing wedge
is signed and centered at 100; fixed and marginal costs have different
comparative statics; split outcomes are configurations; and latent preferred
scale is integrated rather than deterministically assigned.

The record audit also changes the empirical picture. Half of the 52 exact-99
buildings belong to repeated-99 candidate parents, and another seven have
non-99 companions. Those buildings are not generally smaller than the
currently unlinked 99s. But candidate parent evidence is not legal proof of a
successful 485-x split. The immediate bottleneck is the HPD and zoning-lot
crosswalk, followed by a credible wage and net-benefit ledger. Once those
objects exist, the simple model can translate project-level response patterns
into redesign costs, split costs, and sample-conditional developer losses
without prematurely claiming renter or social welfare effects.
