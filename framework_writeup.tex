\begin{abstract}
New York City's 485-x program gives developers a reason to keep separately assessed rental developments below 100 units. Developers can respond by building fewer apartments or by dividing a larger project into smaller parts. We study these choices together to measure how the threshold changes proposed housing supply. The empirical comparison uses earlier projects on similar sites, retaining both their total units and their division across filings. A model of project size and splitting explains why the same incentive can produce 99-unit projects, preserve units through subdivision, or generate pairs of 99-unit constituents. The main quantity of interest is the difference in proposed units among filing opportunities. We estimate the model by maximum likelihood on parents of at most 300 units, backing the costs of the threshold and of splitting out of developers' observed choices. In the main model the threshold's burden differs across developers; it implies about \MainUnitsLost{} fewer proposed units among recent parents (bootstrap interval \MainUnitsLostLower--\MainUnitsLostUpper). Interpreting that difference as a policy effect requires a credible historical comparison, and the estimate depends on how the cost of splitting varies across projects.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question: fewer units or a different project design?}

A concentration of filings at 99 units can reflect two different responses. A developer who reduces a 110-unit project to 99 proposes 11 fewer apartments. A developer who divides the same project into two 55-unit parts preserves all 110. Splitting can also accompany a reduction in size: a 210-unit project divided into two 99-unit parts loses 12 apartments. The housing-supply question therefore requires following the whole development, including its related filings.

We call that development the \emph{parent project}. Its \emph{constituents} are the parts into which it is organized. The data link related new-building filings into parents and retain the size of each constituent. This lets us distinguish a parent's total units from the number of filings it generates.

Under 485-x, rental developments face different affordability requirements and construction compensation rules when the eligible site reaches 100 units. Eligible sites in specified geographic zones face a further regime at 150 units. The statutory unit can be a tax lot or a zoning lot containing several buildings in one application; a separate filing alone does not establish separate treatment \citep[\S1(s), (bb), (ss); \S3; \S8]{nys485x}. We therefore use administrative evidence to assess which observed constituents can be treated separately.

The current comparison places more weight at both 99 and 198 parent units than a historical benchmark adjusted for site characteristics (Table~\ref{tab:evidence}). Every observed 198-unit parent consists of two 99-unit filings. This makes project organization central to the analysis: counting those filings as unrelated opportunities would obscure the scale of the underlying development.

\section{Measuring the change in proposed units}

We compare recent filing opportunities with historical projects on similar sites. Historical parents carry two outcomes together: their proposed total and their observed organization. Reweighting changes how much each historical parent contributes to the comparison; it leaves that parent's outcomes intact. Thus ordinary multi-building development remains part of the benchmark.

Let historical parent $j$ have total units $x_j$, constituent count $J_j^0$, and site characteristics $X_j$ measured from archival parcels. Give it normalized weight $\widetilde w_j$, where $\sum_j\widetilde w_j=1$. Let $P$ be the number of post-period parents in the same comparison sample, with observed totals $m_i$. The direct difference in proposed units is
\begin{equation}
\Delta U_{\mathrm{dist}}
=P\left(\sum_j\widetilde w_jx_j-\frac{1}{P}\sum_{i=1}^{P}m_i\right).
\label{eq:direct}
\end{equation}
A positive value means that the historical benchmark implies more units for the same number of filing opportunities. This calculation needs complete unit totals, including the largest projects. A chart that combines projects above 300 into one bin retains their probability mass but cannot, on its own, measure their contribution to housing supply.

The economic assumption is that, after adjusting for site composition, earlier projects describe what these opportunities would have proposed without the size-dependent escalation. Earlier tax regimes, financing conditions, rents, and construction costs can differ. Balanced site characteristics address differences in observed sites; they do not remove all changes in project economics. Recent pre-policy comparisons help assess whether the benchmark predicts size and organization before the threshold changes.

The estimate is conditional on filing. It measures units proposed within a set of opportunities, while annualized filing counts describe changes in activity separately. It does not recover projects that were cancelled or never filed. The current size comparison also conditions on parents with at least 50 observed units. If the policy moves projects across that sample boundary, the comparison changes membership as well as size. We therefore examine the broader six-plus-unit population when assessing the quantity result. Appendix~\ref{app:data} gives the sample and linkage details.

Equation~\eqref{eq:direct} provides the quantity benchmark. The model below adds an explanation for the change: how much reflects smaller projects, how much reflects different organization, and what the joint response implies about the costs developers face. \path{tasks/estimate_notch_model} produces the structural estimates.

\section{The developer's choice}

\subsection{Total units and the ordinary cost of organization}

A developer chooses total units $m$ and a feasible division $\mathbf n=(n_1,\ldots,n_J)$, with $m=\sum_{k=1}^J n_k$. The parent's land is part of the developer's choice: developers assemble and divide sites, so the ordinary cost of departing from the preferred total includes any change in land. The reweighted historical joint distribution supplies ordinary size and organization, including parents that ordinarily have multiple constituents. Before the modeled escalation, surplus from total units is
\begin{equation}
\pi_{0i}(m)=v_i m-C_{0i}(m),
\qquad C_{0i}(m)=\frac{\alpha_i}{1+\lambda}m^{1+\lambda},
\qquad \alpha_i>0,\quad\lambda>0.
\label{eq:baseline}
\end{equation}
The profitability shifter $v_i$ captures what another unit contributes before the curved cost term. The preferred continuous total is $x_i=(v_i/\alpha_i)^{1/\lambda}$. Curvature $\lambda$ determines how costly it is to depart from that total. The cost function summarizes project economics after other design choices have been made; it need not equal the construction budget. The approach builds on housing-supply models in which regulation raises marginal development cost \citep{brueckner2024}.

Organization also has an ordinary cost. Some sites naturally favor several buildings; others are costly to divide. The historical parent's own organization, $J_i^0$ constituents, reveals what it ordinarily prefers, so we treat that organization as free and charge only for departures from it. Each constituent added beyond $J_i^0$ is costly, and more so for each additional one:
\begin{equation}
\psi_i(J)=c_i\,(J-J_i^0)^{\gamma}\quad\text{for }J\ge J_i^0,
\qquad c_i\sim\text{Exponential with mean }\sigma,
\qquad \gamma\ge1.
\label{eq:organization}
\end{equation}
The cost $c_i$ differs across developers: some have sites, lawyers and designs that make a second building easy, others do not. With $\gamma>1$ a third building costs more than a second, so a parent that splits usually adds one building, which is what produces $99+99$ pairs. Consolidating into fewer constituents than $J_i^0$ is never chosen: it cannot lower the policy burden and gives up the preferred organization. Without the escalation, every parent keeps its historical total and organization, so the reweighted historical distribution is the model's no-policy benchmark exactly.

\subsection{The burden at 100 units}

For a constituent assessed separately under the policy, let the incremental net burden be
\begin{equation}
d_i(n)=
\begin{cases}
0,& n\le99,\\[2pt]
K_i+\dfrac{\beta_i}{1+\lambda}
\left(n^{1+\lambda}-100^{1+\lambda}\right),& n\ge100,
\end{cases}
\qquad K_i,\beta_i\ge0.
\label{eq:burden}
\end{equation}
The level jump $K_i$ makes entry into the high regime costly even for a project that would otherwise choose just over 99. The steeper cost schedule, governed by $\beta_i$, can also reduce the size of projects that remain above the threshold. Both terms represent net effects of policy requirements and benefits. Setting the incremental burden to zero removes the modeled escalation while retaining ordinary project economics, rather than removing the entire tax incentive.

The burden differs across developers. The construction wage floor raises the cost of every construction hour, so a developer whose contractor already pays close to the required wage feels both the jump and the steeper schedule little, and one far below it feels both a great deal. We therefore scale the whole burden by a developer-specific factor $b_i$, lognormal with median one and log standard deviation $s$: developer $i$ faces $b_iK$ and $b_i\beta$, and $K$ and $\beta$ describe the median developer. The factor is independent of the parent's size, site and splitting cost. Making it depend on local construction wages would test the mechanism directly and is reserved for a later version.

For a given total $m$ and constituent count $J$, the developer selects the feasible division with the lowest policy burden:
\begin{equation}
d_{iJ}(m)=\min_{\mathbf n\in\mathcal P_{iJ}(m)}\sum_{k=1}^{J}d_i(n_k).
\label{eq:partition}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal P_{iJ}(m)$ contains the divisions permitted by the site's physical and legal constraints. If separate constituents share a policy obligation, their cost must reflect that joint treatment instead of the additive schedule in \eqref{eq:partition}.

The developer then chooses size and organization together:
\begin{equation}
\max_{m,J}\left\{v_i m-C_{0i}(m)-\psi_i(J)-d_{iJ}(m)\right\}.
\label{eq:choice}
\end{equation}
This comparison allows the developer to reduce total units, change organization, or do both. It also allows the original design to remain optimal.

\subsection{Why 99 and 198 emerge}

For one constituent, 99 is the largest total below the escalation. For two independently assessed constituents, that total is 198, uniquely divided as 99+99. A project that would prefer more than 198 units may stop there if the cost of reducing size is smaller than the burden of crossing the threshold. A project that would prefer 180 units can avoid the escalation through two subthreshold constituents without reducing its total.

Above 198, the developer can put 99 units in one constituent and the remainder in another, or allow both constituents to cross 100. Thus a 220-unit total can be organized as 99+121 or 110+110. Which is cheaper depends on the level jump, the marginal burden, and the site's feasible divisions. The model does not force every large parent into one building or an exact multiple of 99.

Splitting consequently changes what the spikes can identify. A project at 99 may have reduced size because splitting was expensive or unavailable. A project at 198 may have preserved its total or reduced a larger proposal. The distribution of parent totals alone cannot assign a unique origin to each spike. The joint distribution of totals and constituent configurations supplies the additional evidence.

The two-constituent example explains the mechanism. The estimation allows every constituent count up to $\lceil x_i/99\rceil$, the fewest buildings that keep all of them below 100; more buildings cannot lower the burden further. The 150-unit regime in geographic Zones A and B is not modeled for now. It affects only Zone A and B alternatives above 150 units, and restricting observed outcomes below 150 would not remove those alternatives from the developer's choice.

\section{Estimation}

\subsection{Backing costs out of choices}

We never observe the burden of the threshold or the cost of splitting. We infer them from what developers chose, assuming they chose optimally. This is the logic of revealed-preference cost estimation in industrial organization, where firms' prices reveal marginal costs \citep{blp1995}, entry decisions reveal fixed costs \citep{bresnahanreiss1991}, and replacement decisions reveal replacement costs \citep{rust1987}. The closest applications use regulatory thresholds: the response around a notch reveals its cost \citep{klevenwaseem2013,garicano2016}. In those terms the model is a discrete choice with random coefficients on the policy burden ($b_i$) and on the number of added buildings ($c_i$), and no idiosyncratic error.

Costs are expressed relative to each parent's curved cost of 99 units, $A_i=C_{0i}(99)$:
\begin{equation}
\kappa=K/A_i,\qquad
\tau=\beta/\alpha_i,\qquad
\bar c_i=c_i/A_i .
\label{eq:ratios}
\end{equation}
A jump of $\kappa=0.18$ means that crossing 100 costs the median developer 18 percent of the ordinary cost of a 99-unit building. Because profits are not observed, these ratios, not dollars, are what the choices identify, and their level rests on the size-loss curvature $\lambda$, as the level of utility rests on the error variance in a logit. The main estimates set $\lambda=1$; curvature 0.5 and 2 change the level of the costs but not the fit. An outside anchor, such as a pro forma or the industry estimate of the hard-cost increase from prevailing wages, is needed for dollars.

\subsection{From a historical parent to predicted outcomes}

Each weighted historical parent supplies its preferred total $x_j$ and organization $J_j^0$. For given parameters the model gives the probability of each policy-era outcome $(m,J)$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item For each constituent count $J$, find the best total and its cost $R_J$, the size loss plus the lowest burden of any division (Appendix~\ref{app:solution}).
\item The developer with splitting cost $c$ chooses the $J$ that minimizes $R_J+c\,(J-J_j^0)^\gamma$. Each $J$ is a line in $c$, so each is chosen on an interval of $c$, and the exponential distribution gives its probability exactly.
\item Repeat at each value of the burden factor $b$ and average with the lognormal probabilities.
\end{enumerate}
A parent that the burden does not touch keeps its historical outcome with probability one. Weighting each parent's outcome probabilities by its calibration weight and adding them gives the predicted distribution of recent parents over 42 cells: one, two, or three or more constituents, by 14 size bins from 50 to 300 units, with separate bins at 99, 198 and 199. The comparison is among parents of at most 300 units in both periods, renormalized within that range, so the number of very large projects in each period does not enter; a historical parent above 300 that shrinks into the range still counts.

\subsection{Likelihood, grid and bootstrap}

Each recent parent of at most 300 units is treated as a draw from the predicted cell probabilities $p_c(\theta)$. The model assigns probability zero to a few observed outcomes, such as a single building of 105--119 units, which any positive jump makes worse than 99. The likelihood therefore leaves a share $\varepsilon$ of recorded outcomes unexplained, as linkage or unit-count errors would be, spread uniformly over sizes and building groups with shares $u_c$:
\begin{equation}
\log L(\theta,\varepsilon)=\sum_c n_c\log\bigl[(1-\varepsilon)\,p_c(\theta)+\varepsilon\,u_c\bigr],
\label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
where $n_c$ counts recent parents in cell $c$. The unexplained share describes records, not behavior, so units lost use only the model's choices. The parameters $\theta=(\kappa,\tau/\kappa,s,\gamma,\sigma)$ and $\varepsilon$ are chosen on a grid; the kink enters as a ratio to the jump because scaling by $b_i$ preserves that ratio.

Uncertainty comes from 500 bootstrap samples that resample parents with replacement within period and borough, recalibrate the weights to each resampled recent sample, and re-estimate on the full grid. Stratifying by borough keeps Staten Island, with five historical and two recent parents, in every calibration.

\subsection{Units lost}

The model's proposed-unit difference is
\begin{equation}
\Delta U_{\mathrm{model}}
=P\sum_j\widetilde w_j
\left[x_j-\E\{m_j^*\mid x_j,J_j^0\}\right],
\label{eq:modelunits}
\end{equation}
among historical parents whose own size is at most 300, reweighted to sum to one, and scaled to the $P=\ModelPostParents{}$ recent parents in that range. The expectation averages over the burden factor and the splitting cost.

Letting the burden differ across developers improves the likelihood substantially over a single burden. It also beats non-optimizers, the standard alternative \citep{klevenwaseem2013}, and when both are allowed the non-optimizer share goes to zero. The evidence is the region just above 100. A non-optimizer keeps any size, including 101 or 103, where no recent parent sits; a developer with a small burden still drops from 102 to 99, which costs almost nothing, yet stays at 130. Scaling the whole burden, rather than the jump alone, fits the same at its best point and allows a kink.

Making the splitting cost depend on lot area or on project size does not significantly improve the fit ($p=0.07$ and $p=0.15$), but both lean the same way: splitting is harder for the largest projects. Both raise units lost, to \SizeSplittingUnitsLost{} and \LotSplittingUnitsLost{}, because parents that cannot split cheaply shrink instead. How the splitting cost varies with project size is the least settled part of the model and the one units lost depend on most; the headline should carry this range alongside its bootstrap interval.

A logit over strategies---a building count, with every building below 100 or not---in place of the random splitting cost fits worse and has a defect specific to this setting. Each strategy draws its own shock, so once any burden exists a parent just above 100 gains near-equivalent alternatives: at a jump of 0.001, about half of parents wanting 100--110 units would move to 99. Bunching would then reflect the appearance of options rather than their cost. The least-squares single burden (jump \LeastSquaresKappa{}, kink \LeastSquaresTau{}, growth \LeastSquaresGamma{}, mean splitting cost \LeastSquaresSigma{}; \LeastSquaresUnitsLost{} units lost) remains as a robustness specification, estimated also with lot-area weights, a common 180-day follow-up horizon, curvature 0.5 and 2, a 249-unit cutoff, all sizes, and joint assessment of the whole parent.

\subsection{What the model still misses}

The largest misses are not responses to the threshold. Small parents with several buildings, which the policy gives no reason to reorganize, are about half as common after the policy as before, and the model, which leaves them as they were, overpredicts them and three-building parents. The linkage finds such parents equally well in both periods: all of their links carry same-owner evidence, the share is flat across the two DOB systems in 2019--2022, and a common 180-day horizon does not remove the difference. Filings of 6--19 units also became rarer. The model sends every shrinking parent to exactly 99, while some recent parents land at 95--98. It also keeps too many large single buildings.

Two open questions matter most for units lost. Some affected projects may not be built at all, which would raise units lost; or all project sizes may have shifted between periods, which would lower the policy's share of the change. Both would explain fewer large single buildings and more small ones than predicted. Parents first filed in 2026 also split about half as often as 2025 parents on a common short window; the difference is not significant once site size is held fixed, and no rule change explains it, so the periods are pooled.

\subsection{Linking proposals into parent projects}
Historical proposals come from the inactive-inclusive 23Q4 release of the DCP Housing Database, which records designs as of January 2024 and keeps withdrawn and stalled proposals with their status. Recent proposals come from DOB NOW new-building filings, with units from the 25Q4 Housing Database and DOB counts only where the Housing Database is missing. The parent construction links filings within 365 days of the first filing using parcel and site identifiers, historical lot links, explicit project references, corroborated adjacency, and a companion rule: filings by the same owner business or person within 150 meters, or on the same tax block within 200 meters. It applies the same rules across periods. Reviewed links and filing roles are recorded separately. A superseded alternative design remains a source proposal but does not add its units to the parent total.

The main comparison uses 2019--2022 and January 1, 2025--July 8, 2026. Historical parents have complete linkage windows; recent parents enter with complete left-side coverage but can lack a full year of follow-up. Later companion filings can therefore change recent totals and organization. A common 180-day horizon, counting only buildings filed within 180 days of the first filing in both periods, is the robustness check.

The exposure screen identifies plausible taxable rental opportunities under affordability Options A and B. This is a classification of potential exposure, not confirmed program enrollment; those option names are distinct from geographic Zones A and B. Registrations provide positive evidence, while tenure and ownership records help identify developments outside the relevant rental choice. Unresolved legal separation remains a scenario for the structural analysis rather than a confirmed exemption.

\subsection{Reweighting and sample support}
The descriptive parent population begins at six units. The preferred shape comparison begins at 50 parent units and retains all larger parents in its denominator. The reweighted comparison requires complete parcel characteristics and distinct building identifiers among a parent's filings. Historical parcel attributes use lagged MapPLUTO releases; recent attributes use release 23v3.1. Lots that DOF merged into a filing lot within 180 days of the parent's first filing are added in both periods. Documented parcel allocations replace the matched lots for reviewed parents, and six parents carry an approved estimate of earlier building floor. Positive calibration weights match residential floor-area ratio and borough; lot area and existing building density describe the parent's own site, which developers shape by assembling land, and enter only as robustness checks. Constituent organization is a modeled policy response.

The comparison sample contains \CalibrationHistoricalParents{} historical and \CalibrationPostParents{} recent parents. Its weighted historical effective sample size is \CalibrationESS{}. Bootstrap inference re-estimates the weights in each draw and records failed calibrations. Table~\ref{tab:evidence} reports local differences in normalized shares, not counts of projects known to have moved from one size to another.

Exact totals are retained in the underlying parent panel even when figures pool the upper tail. Both quantity formulas use the same target sample and weights. Broadening the sample below 50 requires recomputing that comparison; the existing shape weights should not be applied as though they represented every filing opportunity.

The model does not use a parent's own land. Zoning and borough are treated as fixed traits of the opportunity; lot area is not, because recent parents can assemble less land in response to the threshold. Recent pre-policy holdouts assess whether the weighted comparison reproduces size and organization before the policy change. Floor area, stories, and other physical outcomes provide further evidence where measurement is comparable.

\section{Solving the size and partition problem}
\label{app:solution}

\subsection{Loss from departing from the preferred total}
Using $v_i=\alpha_i x_i^\lambda$, divide the ordinary surplus loss by $A_i=C_{0i}(99)$:
\begin{equation}
\ell_\lambda(m;x)
=\left(\frac{m}{99}\right)^{1+\lambda}
+\lambda\left(\frac{x}{99}\right)^{1+\lambda}
-(1+\lambda)\left(\frac{x}{99}\right)^\lambda\frac{m}{99}.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
This follows from $\pi_0(x)-\pi_0(m)=v(x-m)-C_0(x)+C_0(m)$. For $\lambda=1$, the normalized loss is simply $((m-x)/99)^2$. The normalized constituent burden is
\begin{equation}
\bar d(n)=\ind\{n\ge100\}
\left\{\kappa+\tau\left[\left(\frac{n}{99}\right)^{1+\lambda}
-\left(\frac{100}{99}\right)^{1+\lambda}\right]\right\}.
\end{equation}
The developer minimizes $\ell_\lambda(m;x)+\bar\psi_i(J)+\bar d_{iJ}(m)$ over feasible integer totals and divisions. A bar denotes division by $A_i$. The unknown dollar scale cancels.

\subsection{Two separately assessed constituents}
Suppress the parent index and write $D(n)$ for the high-regime branch of \eqref{eq:burden}. If the relevant divisions are feasible, the minimum burden for two constituents is
\begin{equation}
d_2(m)=
\begin{cases}
0,&m\le198,\\
K,&m=199,\\
\min\{D(m-99),\ D(\lfloor m/2\rfloor)+D(\lceil m/2\rceil)\},&m\ge200.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:two}
\end{equation}
The first case requires a feasible division satisfying both constituents' minimum sizes. At 199, a cost-minimizing division is 99+100. For larger totals, one candidate keeps a constituent at 99; the other divides units as evenly as possible between two high-regime constituents. Convexity delivers the latter result. When burdens are flat, additional cost-minimizing divisions can tie.

For smooth interior branches, put $\theta=(1+\tau)^{1/\lambda}$. The conditional first-order conditions are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{0.40\textwidth}X@{}}
\toprule
Organization & Conditional parent total \\
\midrule
One constituent above 99 & $m=x/\theta$ \\[4pt]
Two constituents below 100 & $m=x$, if feasible and $m\le198$ \\[4pt]
One at 99, one above 99 & $x^\lambda=m^\lambda+\tau(m-99)^\lambda$ \\[4pt]
Two above 99, equal sizes & $m=x/(1+\tau/2^\lambda)^{1/\lambda}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
These formulas describe candidates, not the global choice. The integer calculation compares feasible totals, divisions, and regime boundaries. The 150-unit schedule replaces \eqref{eq:burden} where applicable, including for alternatives not observed in the data.

\subsection{Exact choice probabilities}
For each parent and constituent count $J\ge J^0$ the size search gives the cost $R_J$ of the best total. Totals above $x$ raise both the size loss and the burden, and totals below $99J$ only add loss, so the search covers the integers from $\min(x,99J)$ to $x$. A developer with splitting cost $c$ then pays $R_J+c\,(J-J^0)^\gamma$, a line in $c$ with slope $(J-J^0)^\gamma$. The chosen $J$ follows the lower envelope of these lines: at $c=0$ the lowest $R_J$, and at each crossing the next flatter line, ending at $J^0$. Each $J$ therefore owns an interval $[\underline c_J,\overline c_J)$, and with $c$ exponential with mean $\sigma$ its probability is $e^{-\underline c_J/\sigma}-e^{-\overline c_J/\sigma}$. The burden factor $b$ enters by repeating the calculation at burden levels on a grid extended to 5, where every parent of at most 300 units avoids 100, each level taking the lognormal probability between the midpoints to its neighbors.

\subsection{Larger constituent counts and integer choices}
For unrestricted partitions with minimum constituent size $n_{\min}$, the burden can be computed recursively:
\begin{equation}
d_J(m)=\min_{n_{\min}\le n\le m-(J-1)n_{\min}}
\{d(n)+d_{J-1}(m-n)\},\qquad d_1(m)=d(m),
\end{equation}
with integer $n$. Site restrictions can instead require direct enumeration. Ordinary organization costs must be defined for every allowed $J$, with the historical choice preferred when the escalation is absent. Observed three-plus-constituent parents remain in the analysis.

For an observed historical optimum $k$, exact discrete choice implies
\begin{equation}
C_0(k)-C_0(k-1)\le v\le C_0(k+1)-C_0(k).
\end{equation}
Setting $v=C_0'(k)$ is a convenient anchoring convention consistent with these inequalities. Rounding a continuous policy optimum is not a substitute for comparing integer profits. When layouts tie, the no-escalation calculation retains the historical vector; otherwise exact tied vector frequencies require a separate account of layout preferences.

\section{Organization costs and identification}
\label{app:costs}

The historical organization $J^0$ is taken as the parent's ordinary preference and is free; the policy can only make additional constituents worthwhile. This restriction reproduces historical totals and organization exactly when the escalation is removed, which is the model's benchmark. It rules out consolidation, which cannot lower the separately assessed burden, and it attributes every added constituent to the policy.

What the historical choices do not reveal is how costly it is to add a constituent. The exponential distribution of $c_i$ and the growth $\gamma$ are therefore restrictions, and the quantity estimates depend on them. Three results bound that dependence. First, the growth $\gamma$ is not identified: the bootstrap interval covers the whole grid from 1 to 3. Second, letting the mean splitting cost vary with lot area or project size changes units lost by up to a factor of two without a significant change in fit (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Third, a logit over strategies in place of the exponential cost fits worse and makes bunching respond to the availability of alternatives rather than their cost.

The model's other restrictions are the independence of the burden factor, the splitting cost and the parent's size; the separately assessed schedule, with joint assessment of the whole parent as a robustness case; and the unexplained share, spread uniformly over outcomes. Numerical checks confirm that historical totals and organization are recovered when the escalation is zero, that a single building shrinks to 99 exactly when its preferred total is below $99(1+\sqrt\kappa)$ and is compressed by $1+\tau$ above, that minimum burdens match exhaustive partitions, that the menu of constituent counts never binds, and that the exact probabilities match simulated cost draws.
