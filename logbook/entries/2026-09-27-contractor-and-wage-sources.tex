\section*{September 27, 2026: Contractor permits and prevailing wage schedules}
\textit{Agent-drafted research entry.}

The scaled burden lets each developer feel the wage rule to a different
degree. A developer whose contractor already pays union wages should barely
feel it. Jacob asked for the two public sources needed to measure that, as
acquisition tasks; neither is used by the model yet.

\paragraph{Who built each project.} The DOB job filings we use name the owner
and the filing architect or engineer (every applicant is licensed RA or PE),
not the builder. DOB's permit datasets name the permit holder, almost always a
licensed general contractor. \path{fetch_dob_permits} captures, as of
September 27, the New Building permits of the BIS Permit Issuance dataset
(267,115 rows for 95,524 jobs) and every General Construction permit in DOB
NOW: Build -- Approved Permits (210,929 rows). A scratch match to the comparison
sample found a contractor for 488 of the 588 historical parents (83 percent)
but only 77 of the 304 recent parents (25 percent): permits are issued months
after filing, so 2026 filings are almost all still unpermitted, and the
recent parents with permits are the faster-moving ones. The match should use
license numbers; BIS cuts business names at 25 characters.

\paragraph{Union rates.} \path{fetch_nyc_prevailing_wage_schedules} captures
the Comptroller's construction prevailing wage schedules for July 2018 through
June 2026, and \path{stage_nyc_prevailing_wage_schedules} reads them into
2,157 rates. The rates are union rates, citywide. In 2020--21 a commercial
building carpenter earned \$54.00 an hour plus \$46.88 in benefits and a mason
tender \$38.40 plus \$31.04, well above the 485-x floor of \$40, so a union
contractor's burden should be close to zero. The schedules do not vary by site,
so variation across developers has to come from whether their contractor pays
union rates, not from the schedules. The current year's schedule is amended
during the year and was not captured.
